# The vice-principal's desk

_2003–2016, the kyōtō years_

I became kyōtō, vice-principal, in two thousand three. At a school in Higashisumiyoshi, and then one more after that, and I retired in two thousand sixteen.

People imagine the vice-principal is the one who scolds. No. The vice-principal is the one who knows where everything is. The keys, the budget, the bus reservations, the roof access, which parent must be telephoned before the letter goes out and which parent must receive the letter first and be telephoned after. My desk faced the door. The principal's office was behind me with a door that closed, and my desk faced the door, so that everything that came into the school came past me. Every single thing.

And I will tell you a shameful thing, which is that the work I loved most in thirty-eight years was the timetable. Making the class schedule for the year. You have forty-one teachers and their subjects and their part-time days and Mr. So-and-so who cannot do first period because of his mother, and the special support classes, and the gymnasium which two classes want at once — and there is exactly one arrangement, or two, that make it all fit. And when it fits. Ah. I would do it over three nights in February with the windows dark and a thermos, and when the last block went in I felt — I have never felt anything like it. Not at my wedding. I'm sorry, that's a dreadful thing to say into a machine.

Now. Sakai-san from the Board of Education came to see me in two thousand eight, and again in two thousand eleven, about sitting the principal's examination. They wanted women. They were counting women by then, there were targets. And I declined. Twice. The first time I said my mother-in-law's affairs, which was not untrue, and the second time I said my husband's health, which was — his health was fine, he had a knee. So that was a lie. I said it anyway.

I have told people over the years that I hadn't the strength for it. That a principal must stand in front of three hundred families and be the face, and I am not a face, I am a pair of hands. That is what I say. And there is some truth in it, I'm not — but I notice that when I say it people nod very sympathetically and the conversation ends, and I have never once corrected the impression, so perhaps I should examine that. Another time.

The children. People ask, didn't you want children, and I say the school gave me hundreds. Which sounds like a line from a poster, but. There was a boy, Kawabata, third year, who stopped coming in the November, and I went to the apartment block in the evening four times before anyone opened, and the mother was — the situation was bad, I won't go into it. And we got him through. He came to the graduation. He's forty now, he sends a New Year card, he does something with trucks. And that was enough. Truly. It was quite enough, more than enough, and I am tired of the faces people make when I say it, as though I am being brave.

The hardest year was two thousand eleven. Fujita-sensei — I mentioned her, the home economics teacher, we had been together at the Nishinari school and then again later — she was ill from the spring and she died in the October. In Wakayama, that's where her family were. And it rained at the funeral, one of those rains that comes sideways, and there were persimmons on a tree in the garden of the house, orange, extremely orange against all that grey, and her sister kept apologizing for the road. And I held her hand in the hospital in the September and her hand was — she had burned her wrist once on a steam tray, years before, there was a mark, and I kept looking at the mark.

I conducted a staff meeting the following Tuesday about the sports festival equipment. Nobody knew. That is not a complaint. I did not want anybody to know. I simply — I have thought since that I did my best work that autumn, the timetable that February was the best one I ever made, and I don't know what to do with that information. I've never said it out loud until just now.
