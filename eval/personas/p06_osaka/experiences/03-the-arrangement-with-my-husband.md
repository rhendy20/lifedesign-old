# The arrangement with my husband

_1981–2000s, marriage_

It was a miai. An arranged introduction. Nineteen eighty-one, in a hotel in Umeda, and there was a photograph album and his mother did most of the talking. His name was Hiroshi. He was an engineer with the municipal waterworks bureau, and I remember that when it was his turn to speak he explained, at some length, the filtration system at the Kunijima plant. And the go-between was making faces at him to stop. But I — I liked it. I thought, here is a man who will tell me the truth about water and will not tell me anything about himself, and I thought that would be restful. And it was restful. For forty years it was restful.

We married the following spring. His mother came to live with us in eighty-nine after his father died and she was with us eleven years, and — well, I nursed her at the end, and I will say only that we came to an understanding somewhere around year six, and that understanding was that she managed the kitchen and I managed everything outside the door, and we did not cross. At her funeral I cried, which surprised me.

We were not — ah. How to say this. The question of children. It didn't. We didn't. In the early years there was, you know, the expectation, and his mother would say things sideways, about the Nakamuras' grandchild and so forth, and I would say oh, is that so, how nice. And then I was thirty-five and then I was forty and then it was simply not a question anyone asked anymore, and I confess the day I noticed that nobody had asked me in a year was — it was a Tuesday, I remember, and I bought a very good melon on the way home. I don't know why I remember the melon.

But you asked about him. Hiroshi. He fished. Every Sunday from about nineteen ninety onward he went to Wakayama or to the Yodo River with two men from the bureau and he came back at four in the afternoon smelling of the sun, and he would clean the rods on newspaper in the genkan. And Sunday was — those were my days. I graded, I read, I went to the department store and looked at things I did not buy, I sat in the house with all the windows open and no one in it. I have never told anyone that Sunday was my favorite day and that it was my favorite day because he was not there. That sounds terrible. It is not terrible. He would have said the same about the river.

We spoke, of course. About the rain, about his knee, about whether the hedge wanted cutting. In forty years I do not believe he asked me one question about my work that was not about the commute. And I did not ask him about the bureau. When he was passed over for section chief in ninety-four I learned about it from his colleague's wife at a New Year gathering, two months after. I said nothing to him. I made a better dinner that week. That was how we did it. That was the language we had and it was a real language, I want to be clear, it was not nothing, it was simply not the language in the television dramas.

My friend Fujita-sensei, the home economics teacher, she was — she used to say, Yoshida-san, does your husband know you at all. And I would say, he knows the hedge wants cutting. And she would laugh. She had that laugh where she put her hand flat on the table.

Ah — I've gone off. Sorry.

What shaped me about it, I suppose. I think it is that I learned a person can be happy inside a shape that from the outside looks like a compromise. Everyone assumed. My younger colleagues assumed I was enduring something. There was a young woman, Ōtsuka-sensei, around two thousand five, who said to me once, very kindly, she said, sensei, you have given up so much. And I was so — I was quite angry, actually, which is not like me. I said, thank you for your concern.

I have given up things. Of course. But not the ones she meant. Not one of the ones she meant.
