# The transfer I did not mention

_1978–1989, first postings_

I became a teacher because it was one of the three things a woman could become, that's really the whole of it, don't let me pretend otherwise. Teacher, nurse, or the bank until you married. I chose social studies, which was slightly unusual, the women were mostly in home economics or Japanese, and my faculty adviser asked me twice if I was certain. I said I was certain. I was not certain about very much else but I was certain about that.

First posting, nineteen seventy-eight, a junior high in Suminoe. Now — the tea. You will have heard about the tea. Every morning the women teachers made the tea, and at the staff meetings we poured it, and after the meeting we collected the cups and washed them, and this was not written anywhere, it was simply the shape of the air. And I did it. For twelve years I did it. I want to be honest, I did not object. I have read things since where women say they objected and perhaps they did, but I did not. I poured the tea and I thought about other matters.

There was a man, Kuroda-sensei, two years younger than me. In my fourth year I had prepared — the third-year homeroom, the one that takes the students through the high school entrance examinations, which is the serious work, that was to come to me, everyone assumed it, I had made the whole plan, the interview schedule for the parents, everything, in a folder. And in March the principal called me in and said Kuroda-kun will take the third year, and Yoshida-sensei will support him, because a young man needs the experience, and because — he actually said this — because you may have circumstances. Meaning I might marry, I might become pregnant. I was twenty-six.

I said, I understand. Thank you for telling me directly. And I gave Kuroda-kun the folder. The whole folder, with the parent schedule and everything. He used it for six years, I believe.

Now. People hear that and they think I was, ah, crushed, or that I went home and wept. I did not. What I did — and this is the part that I think is more true about me — is I began to read the transfer regulations. The municipal transfer system. Very dull documents. And I learned that there was a category, schools designated as requiring particular support, difficult schools, and that a teacher could volunteer for those, and that almost nobody volunteered, and that if you volunteered you were not, ah, you were not handed things, you were simply needed. You walked in and you were needed on the first morning.

So in the autumn of eighty-eight I filed the request. By then I was married — I married in eighty-two — and I filed it and I did not mention it to my husband. Not because he would have forbidden it. He was not that kind of man at all. I think I simply — I did not want the conversation. I wanted the thing to be done and then to be told about. So it was decided in February and that evening at dinner I said, I'll be moving to a school in Nishinari in April, the commute is forty minutes. And he put down his chopsticks and said, ah, is that so. Forty minutes. And then he asked whether I would need to change at Tennōji. That was the entire discussion of it. He asked about the transfer at Tennōji.

People say that is sad. I have had younger women tell me that is sad. I did not find it sad. I found it — I don't know the word. It was the arrangement we had and it suited us and I would not have wanted him poking about in it, asking me was I sure, did I feel it was wise. God. No.

The Nishinari school. That was eight years and they were — the windows were broken more than once and a boy threatened me with a chair, and I would not trade those eight years for anything. Because nobody there had time to think about who poured the tea. There was too much to do. You know, when a building is on fire nobody asks whether the woman may hold the hose.

And I made the tea there too, actually. I did. But I made it because I wanted it, and there is a difference, and it is not a difference you can explain to anybody, and I've stopped trying.
