# My grandmother's notebook

_1963–1965, Ikuno Ward, age eight or nine_

Where shall I begin. Well — the house. It was a nagaya, a row house, in Ikuno, and if you sneezed the Tanakas next door would say bless you through the wall, that sort of place. My father worked a metal press. He crushed two fingers on his left hand in, I think it was the autumn of nineteen sixty-three, and after that he was home for a long while, and he sat. That is what I remember, that he sat. He was not a cruel man. He simply sat with his hand wrapped and looked at the alley.

So it was my grandmother who — my father's mother, she lived with us — she did piecework, sewing. Trouser hems, mostly, and later some kind of collar for a uniform company. The machine was by the window because that was where the light was. And her hands, I can still, ah, I can still smell them, is that a strange thing to say. Machine oil and rice starch. When she put her hand on the back of my neck it was cool and it smelled of that.

But the notebook. That is really the thing. She kept a notebook — well, several, but there was one, a cheap one, the cover was a sort of persimmon color, and in it she wrote what was given and what was owed. Not money exactly. I mean money also, but. When someone in the lane had a funeral, what we took. When someone had a baby, what came. Mrs. Kishi brought three eggs on the day my father was injured, and that went in. Three eggs, Kishi-san, October. My grandmother had very little schooling but her writing was — she wrote the names beautifully. I learned my kanji from that notebook, honestly, more than from school. I would sit beside the machine and copy the names.

And the Kishi family — this is the part. Mr. Kishi's son got into some trouble, I never knew exactly what, there were men who came round, and one morning the Kishis were gone. Simply gone, in the night, the way people did then. And there was a line in the notebook. Money my grandmother had lent Mrs. Kishi, two thousand yen I think, which was not nothing, and now the line just — sat there. Open. And my grandmother said, well, that's that, and she didn't cross it out and she didn't do anything, she just went on hemming trousers.

And I could not bear it. I want to be careful, I'm not saying I was a good child. It wasn't kindness. It was that the line was open and I couldn't look at the page. I found out from a girl at school that the Kishis had gone to Hirano, near the market, and I took the money — I had money, from New Year, and from running errands, I had kept it in a tin, I was a saving sort of child — and one Sunday I said I was going to the shrine and I walked. It took me two hours, I think, I got lost near the river. And I found the place, and I gave the envelope to Mrs. Kishi and said my grandmother sends this back, which was — that was a lie, obviously. And Mrs. Kishi cried and I was extremely uncomfortable and I left.

I told no one. For about a month. And then my grandmother — she must have heard something from someone, the lane was like that — she called me over one evening and she opened the notebook and she wrote in it. Kishi-san, settled, Sumie. And then she closed it and gave me a rice cracker and we did not discuss it. Not once, ever, not in her whole life. She died in seventy-one.

My brother, by the way, got a new futon that winter and I did not, and I remember being furious about that, which shows you what a child is. Ha. And we had a canary at some point, I don't know where the canary came from.

But, ah, what was I — yes. I think that's the thing I would say made me. Not the poverty. Everyone was poor, that isn't a story. It's that I understood very young that there is an account being kept, of what people have done for each other, and that somebody has to keep it, and that mostly nobody thanks that person, and that the keeping of it is — it is not a burden if you have the temperament. I had the temperament. I have never in my life been comfortable with an open line.
