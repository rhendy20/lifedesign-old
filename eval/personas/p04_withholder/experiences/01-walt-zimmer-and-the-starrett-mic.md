# Walt Zimmer and the Starrett mic

_1983-1985, first shop job_

Okay. Uh. So — you want, what, a thing that, alright.

First real shop I was in was off East 12th, right down by where the tracks cross. Building's gone now, it's a gravel lot, they use it for trailers. Eighty-three. I was eighteen. We ran hydraulic fittings mostly, some valve bodies for a outfit in Cleveland.

There was an old guy there, Walt Zimmer. Big fella. He had the two Monarchs on the end and nobody else touched 'em. And I was, you know, I was sweeping. That's what you did. You swept and you deburred and you kept your mouth shut. And he didn't say nothin' to me for probably four months. Then one day he come over and he took the part outta my hand and he set it down and he says, you're holding the mic wrong, you're gonna feel it wrong your whole life. And he showed me. It's the ratchet, you let the ratchet do it, three clicks, you don't muscle it. Three clicks and you stop.

He was, uh. He had the lung thing. From the — everybody had it, the mist, the coolant, nobody wore nothin' back then. And he went out in, I wanna say September, and he was gone by Thanksgiving.

His wife brought a box in and said take what you want, and everybody stood there. So I took the mic. Starrett 436, one inch, it's carbide faces, it's got his initials scratched in the frame with a vibro pen, W.E.Z. Still got it. It's in the second drawer of the top box. I checked it against the gage blocks maybe two years ago, still dead on, no wear on the anvil that I can find. Forty-some years.

The stone at Trinity there, the lettering on it, the — that got taken care of. The local, I guess, or somebody. That's how it went then.

And, uh. That's — I mean the way he set that part down. I think about that. He didn't say it mean. He just set it down.

Anyway. That's the shop. They closed in eighty-seven.
