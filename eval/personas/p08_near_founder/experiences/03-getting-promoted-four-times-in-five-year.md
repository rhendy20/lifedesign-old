# Getting promoted four times in five years

_Ages 24–30, at the startup in Lehi_

So, uh, the startup. This is the part of my resume people actually care about, ironically, not the part I care about.

I got hired as like rep six. Six or seven. Scheduling software for home service guys, plumbers, HVAC, garage doors. And I knew nothing about software but I knew what it was like to sit in a truck with a guy who's trying to figure out where he's going at 7am, right, because I grew up in that truck. So my demos were just, like, I'd talk to these owners about their day and they'd go "you get it" and they'd buy. First full year I hit 143% of quota, which was second in the company, and Enrique beat me but Enrique had the Texas territory which was, that's a gift, that territory was a gift.

And then, whatever, AE to senior AE to team lead to running customer success to director of CS. Four titles in five years. Which sounds great and honestly a lot of it was just that the company was doubling, like, if you just don't leave and you're not bad you go up.

The leaderboard was on a TV by the kitchen. And then they moved it into Salesforce, into a dashboard, and I — okay this is embarrassing but I would check it at like 5:40 in the morning. In bed. Before Britt woke up. Because the overnight sync ran at 4 and the numbers would be fresh and I'd just want to see it. I wasn't even, like, it wasn't stress, it was almost like a — it was the fax again. It's the same thing as the fax.

When I took over CS I had eleven people eventually. And I want to say, like, I'm a big believer in autonomy. I really am. I hated being micromanaged, I had a manager for about a year, Doug, good dude, and he wanted to do these ride-alongs where he'd sit in on my calls and I — I never did one. In a year. I had a reason every time and they were all legitimate reasons and I never did one. So I know what that feels like and I don't do that to my people.

But, um. Okay. Brightway Plumbing. Hundred and eighty grand a year, our third biggest account, and it was Kaylee's account, Kaylee was great, she was maybe six months in. And they had a renewal coming and their ops manager was making noises. And I took it. I just took it off her. I told her it was so she could focus on her book, and that was true, her book was heavy. And I ran that renewal myself and we saved it, we actually upsold it, 218.

And she quit like four months later. And in the exit thing she said something about not being trusted, which I heard secondhand, and I remember thinking, that's not — that's not what that was. That account was too important to learn on.

And I mean I was doing everybody's QBR decks. At eleven at night. Like I'd rebuild them. I'd take their deck and rebuild it and send it back at 11:40 pm and say "just a couple tweaks!" with an exclamation point. Every time. And I had this — I had a spreadsheet, my own one, not the company one, where I tracked every CSM's touches per account per week. Color coded. Nobody asked me for it. I just wanted to know.

The thing I'm actually proud of from those years — we had this renewal quarter, Q3 of, uh, 2019 I think, where churn was gonna be brutal because of a pricing change, and I put every single at-risk account on a wall. Physical wall. Sticky notes, like a giant one, and I stood in front of it every morning at 7:15 with whoever was in the office and we just went account by account. And we ended at like 94% gross retention when the model said 88.

My wife came to the Christmas party that year and somebody told her I was the reason the company didn't fall apart and I could tell she didn't totally believe it, which, fair. She sees me at home. At home I can't find my keys.

But yeah. That wall. I loved that wall. I've got a wall now at my own place, smaller, but same idea. Sticky notes. My ops guy thinks it's hilarious, he keeps trying to get me onto a tool and I'm like, no, buddy. The wall stays.
