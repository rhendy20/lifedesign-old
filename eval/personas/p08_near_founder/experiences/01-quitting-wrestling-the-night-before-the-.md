# Quitting wrestling the night before the wrestle-off

_Age 15, sophomore year of high school_

Okay so. Um. I think the one that people don't— I don't really tell this one much, honestly, but it's probably, I dunno. It's probably the one. So I wrestled. Spanish Fork, growing up, and wrestling is just kind of what you did, my older brother did it, my dad did it back when, and I was — I was decent. Like I wasn't a state kid but I was decent. Sophomore year I went 23 and 4. Which, you can go look it up, it's in the — I mean it was in the program. 23 and 4, and two of the losses were to the same kid from Payson, Ayala, who ended up placing third at state, so like, contextually, that's a fine record. That's a good record for a sophomore.

And there was this thing where, uh, so I wrestled 140 and there was Tanner Bigelow at 145, and Tanner was a junior, and he was — I mean he was good, he was strong, but he was lazy, he was, he'd skip the morning lifts constantly. And coach Nielsen, coach was gonna do a wrestle-off, because I was gonna bump up, because the 140 spot was, whatever, it doesn't matter, the point is there was gonna be a wrestle-off on a Tuesday to see who took varsity 145 for the rest of the season.

And I quit on the Monday.

And like — I've told this a bunch of ways over the years. The way I've usually told it is that my dad had the shop, he did HVAC, residential mostly, and he needed somebody Saturdays, and Saturdays was when we had tournaments, and I had kind of already decided I wanted to be making money, I wanted a truck, I wanted— and that's all true. That's genuinely all true, I did start working Saturdays that same week, I made like six bucks an hour handing him stuff and sweeping and I loved it. I did. I loved it more than wrestling probably.

But, I mean, it was the Monday. I'm aware. I'm aware of when it was.

And I remember, um. I remember I told coach after practice, in the hall by the mat room where it smells like — you know that smell, that mat cleaner smell, I can still, yeah. And he just said, "Okay, Rasmussen." That's it. That's all he said. He didn't fight me on it at all and I think — I don't know, I think I wanted him to fight me on it a little? Like I think I'd kind of pictured him going, no, hang on, you're my guy. And he just said okay and went and got his clipboard.

Tanner went 8 and 14 that year at varsity, by the way. Eight and fourteen. I checked. Like I actively went and found out, which, that's a weird thing for a fifteen-year-old to do but I did.

The thing I keep coming back to is the truck rides with my dad after. Because that whole rest of that winter, Saturdays, we'd drive to Payson or Elk Ridge or wherever, and my dad is not — he doesn't talk. Like he's not a talker. But he had this thing where in the truck he'd go through the jobs. Like, out loud. "Okay, Hendersons, that's a coil, that's two hours, then we got the duct thing in Salem, that's, ah, that's probably nothing, that's a fifteen minute look." Just — ordering the day. And I'd sit there with the clipboard and write the times in. And that was, I'm not kidding, that was the closest I ever felt to my dad, was doing that. We didn't talk about anything real, ever. Not once. He never asked me why I quit. Never brought it up, not then, not ever, I mean he's still alive, he's in Lehi, we still haven't talked about it.

But he let me hold the clipboard.

And I dunno, I think I learned — I think what I took from that period is like, I'd rather be the guy who's building the day than the guy waiting to find out what the day is. If that makes sense. Like the mat room, you're just standing there in a circle waiting for coach to call your name and then you go do the thing he says. And in the truck it's, okay, here's the list, here's the order, let's go. I just liked that so much better. I've always been like that.

Sorry, that was — that's probably longer than you wanted. But yeah. Twenty-three and four. I still think I would've beat Tanner. I mean, I think I would've. He had like eleven pounds on me but he gassed out, he always gassed out in the third. So.
