# The numbers board in Brazil

_Ages 19–21, mission in southern Brazil_

Um, so the mission. Yeah. Everybody says the mission changed them and it's like, okay, but it actually did, so.

I was in Paraná, south of Brazil, Londrina, Maringá, a bunch of smaller places. Two years. And the thing that — okay so the thing everybody expects you to say is the baptism story and I do have one, there was this guy Everton, he had a bakery, tiny place, and he'd been, um, he'd had a really rough thing happen with his brother, and we taught him and he got baptized and he's still active, he's got kids now, we're friends on Facebook. He sends me a message every single New Year's. Every year. Which is amazing. So yeah, that was, that was real, that mattered.

But if I'm being honest about what actually formed me it was the board.

So every Sunday night you call in numbers to your district leader. Lessons taught, new investigators, member presents, baptismal dates. And your district leader calls it into the zone leaders and they call it to the assistants and it all rolls up. And then Wednesday you'd get this fax — a fax, this is 2012, we had a fax machine at the chapel — with every district in the mission and their numbers. And I would — oh my gosh. I would wait for that fax.

And I was made district leader at like eight months, which was fast, and I got kind of, uh, I got a little obsessed. I made my own board. On the back of our apartment door with masking tape, a grid, every companionship in my district, every day, every number. My comp at the time was Elder Whitmore, good guy, he could not get out of bed, like it was a genuine physical problem for him, and I had this — I'd write his wake-up time on the door. Every day. In marker. And he'd laugh about it but I don't think he thought it was funny. I think about that sometimes.

And we went from like, our district was doing four lessons a week to like twenty-six. Twenty-six with member present, which in Paraná at that time was — that was a lot. And I was number one on that fax for I want to say nine weeks straight. Nine. And I'd read it in the bathroom because it felt weird to be that happy about it in front of everybody.

Okay so here's the part I think about. About eighteen months in, one of the assistants, Elder Pacheco, he tells me at a zone conference, kind of sideways, like — "the president's been talking about you." And I knew what that meant. Zone leader, maybe assistant, whatever. And you're not supposed to want it. You're really not supposed to want it. And I, uh.

So transfers were two weeks out and I wrote the president a letter. And I asked him to send me to Ivaiporã. Which is this tiny, tiny branch in the middle of nowhere, like sixty members, one of the elders there had gone home early and it was a mess. And I wrote this whole thing about how I wanted to just be a missionary, I didn't want a title, I wanted to work. And I meant it! Like when I read it back in my journal I meant every word, it's not fake.

But I also — I mean, I sent that letter before transfer calls. Two weeks before. And I remember sitting on the floor of the apartment when the calls came through and they read the zone leader assignments and my name wasn't in it, and I felt — this is gonna sound bad — I felt so relaxed. Like my shoulders came down. Physically. And I thought at the time that was the Spirit confirming it. That's what I wrote in my journal, I wrote "peace."

And Ivaiporã was great, genuinely, that's where I learned Portuguese for real, that's where I ate dinner with the same family like forty times, Irmã Solange, she made this pastel thing. That's where Everton was, actually, no wait, Everton was Maringá. Whatever.

But yeah, I ran that branch. Not — I mean, not officially, the branch president ran it, Irmão Cleber, super nice guy, but he worked nights and, um, I kind of ended up making the ward council agenda. And doing the records. And I built a board there too. On the back of a door, again. Masking tape, again.

My trainer used to say "Elder, você conta tudo." You count everything. He meant it as a compliment I'm pretty sure.
