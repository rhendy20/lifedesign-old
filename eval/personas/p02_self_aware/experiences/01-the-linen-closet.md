# The linen closet

_Age 10–12, Aurora, 1998–2000_

Okay. Um. So I always start with the same year, which my therapist would say is meaningful, and she's probably right. I was ten. My parents split in — it was the spring of ninety-eight, I remember because of the Elway thing, everybody in Aurora was insane about the Broncos and meanwhile our house was, you know. Not that. My dad moved to an apartment in Lakewood with popcorn ceilings and a hot tub that was always broken, and my mom stayed in the house on Dahlia with me and my sister Kelsey and the beagle. Sergeant. He was a terrible dog, he ate a whole loaf of bread once, wrapper and all. Anyway.

So the thing everybody says about that year, the story I tell, is that my mom made me her confidant. Which — yes. She did. She would sit at the kitchen table with the little glass of white wine and she'd tell me about the money, about Debra, who was the woman, about how my dad had said this or that in mediation. And I was ten. I'd sit there in my nightgown and I would say the right things. I got very good at the right things. My therapist used the phrase emotional incest one time and I literally, like, physically recoiled, and then I went home and journaled about it for an hour because obviously she was right. Parentified kid. Classic. I could put it on a t-shirt.

But that's — okay, here's the part I don't usually lead with. My mom kept a journal. One of those fabric-covered ones from the Hallmark store, like a paisley thing. And she kept it in the linen closet in the upstairs hall, underneath the flat sheets, which is a wild hiding place for a person with two children who do laundry. And I found it, I want to say that September, and I read it. And then I kept reading it. For like two years. I had a — I'd wait until she was at work, and Kelsey was at swim, and I'd take it into the bathroom and lock the door and read the new pages. I got so I could tell how many pages were new just by the — by the thickness, kind of, by where the ribbon was.

And I want to be really clear that this was awful for me. It was. I knew that my grandpa had loaned her eleven thousand dollars. I knew she'd thought about leaving in nineteen ninety-four. I knew she found me difficult, which — there's a whole entry about how I'm difficult, quote, in a way Kelsey isn't. I was eleven, reading that. Hypervigilance, right? That's where the hypervigilance comes from. I've done so much work on that. EMDR, the whole thing.

But, um. What I did with it. So I'd know, like, I'd know on a Tuesday that Thursday was the day the lawyer was calling. So Thursday I'd come home and I'd have already started dinner. Or I'd know she was spiraling about Debra so I'd be extra — I'd be funny that week. I was very funny that year. And she'd say, you just know me, you're my person, you just know. And Kelsey didn't know any of it. Kelsey was out riding her bike being a normal ten — nine-year-old. I never told her. I've still never told her. I'm forty — I'm thirty-eight and my sister does not know that I read our mother's diary for two years and honestly I think about it at like, weird times. Like at a red light.

And the other thing from that time, and this is silly, but — I couldn't sleep. So I would get up at eleven, midnight, and I would go down and unload the dishwasher and wipe the counters and put the cereal boxes back in the cabinet in order. Like tall to short. And my mom would come down at six and go, oh, the kitchen fairy came. And it just — I don't know, I liked that. Over-functioning starts young, is I guess what I'd say. That's my whole thing, right? I take care of it before anybody has to ask. My therapist and I have a lot of material there.

Um, my dad died in 2019, by the way. Pancreatic. And I feel like I should have more to say about that and I don't, really, which I've processed. We weren't — it was fine. I was fine. I flew out for the service and I was the one who organized the funeral lunch and got the potatoes, because of course I was.

Anyway. That's the one I always start with.
