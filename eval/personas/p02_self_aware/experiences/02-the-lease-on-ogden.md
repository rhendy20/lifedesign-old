# The lease on Ogden

_Age 24, Capitol Hill, 2012_

So Jeremy. Um. This is the one that I think actually — I mean they all shaped me, but this one I can point at.

I was twenty-four, I had my first real HR job, like coordinator level, benefits enrollment, riveting. And I'd been with Jeremy about two years. He was a — he did something with soil, he was a hydrologist, he had those, like, hiking sandals I made fun of relentlessly. Genuinely one of the kindest humans I have ever been in a room with. Everybody says that about him. His mom sent me a card for two years after.

And we were going to move in together. We had the lease, it was a one-bedroom on Twelfth and Ogden, it had a claw-foot tub and no closet, and we'd put down the deposit. We had, like, a spreadsheet for the furniture.

And I ended it three weeks before we signed.

And the way I understand it now — and I've done years on this, I could give you the whole formulation — I have abandonment stuff. Like, deep. My dad left, my mom emotionally leaned on me instead of being a parent, and so I have this thing where the second something is real I get certain that it's going to be taken, and so I take it first. I leave before I'm left. Textbook. I mean it's so textbook that when I said it out loud in my Theories class last year three people wrote it down.

But. Okay. The actual moment. It was Thanksgiving, we drove up to his parents' in Loveland, and his sister — Katie — she's hugging me in the driveway before I've even got my bag out, and she goes, oh my god, we feel like we already know you, Jeremy has told us everything about you.

And I laughed. And I was pleasant all weekend. I made a pie. And I remember standing in their kitchen and something in me had just — gone. Like a breaker flipped. And I drove home on I-25 that Sunday and I did not say one word for an hour and he kept going, are you okay, you're quiet, and I said I was just tired.

And then for like eight days I couldn't sleep and I'd get in the car at eleven at night and drive up 70 to Idaho Springs and turn around and come back. Just — like ninety minutes. Radio off, usually. I did that a lot that winter. I don't know what that was. Decompressing, probably.

And then three weeks later I sat him down on the futon and did it, and the thing that I still — he cried, and he said, I don't understand, and I gave him this whole articulate speech about how I wasn't ready and I needed to grow and I had work to do on myself. Which, I mean. That was true. But I said it so well. I've thought about that. I was twenty-four and I delivered it like a closing argument and he didn't have anything to say back to it because how do you argue with somebody doing work on themselves.

Oh — and I have to say this because it's, um. Before that. Like six months before. I read his email. He left his laptop open at my place and I read, like, two years of his email. Including stuff with his ex, including what he told his best friend about me. And there was nothing bad in it. That's the thing, there was genuinely nothing, he'd written to Sam that I was, like, the real deal. And I sat there at two in the morning on my floor reading this man being lovely about me and I just — I don't know. I felt very calm, actually. I felt like I had my footing back.

I'm not proud of that. I'm not, like, a stalker. I just — I get very uncomfortable when I don't know where people are, mentally. Like where they are with me. And I kept tabs on him for probably a year after through a mutual friend's Instagram, which — you know, grief. That's grief.

He's married now, they've got two kids, they live in Fort Collins. Not that I've — I mean, you see things.

But yeah. That was the first time I really understood that I do the leaving. Which is a hard thing to know about yourself but I'd rather know it. I'd so much rather know it than be one of those people wandering around going, why does this keep happening to me.
