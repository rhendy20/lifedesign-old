# The boy with the knife on the 5119

_2021, age 39_

This is a work story. It's the one everyone at the garage knows so I might as well.

So, 2021, we were still in the pandemic thing, I was doing the night, the 5119, which goes out to the end of everything, and the last one is twenty past eleven from the terminal. Night buses are a different animal. Daytime you're a driver, night you're — I don't know what you are. A lighthouse. You've got a nurse, you've got a security guard, you've got a drunk who's very polite, you've got a woman who's been crying since Santo Amaro and everybody's pretending not to see.

And this kid gets on at, it's after the viaduct, and I clocked him immediately. Not because of the face, the face was nothing, he was a kid, maybe nineteen, big ears, he had one of those thin jackets. It was the shoulders. When a person gets on a bus and their shoulders are up by their ears, something is happening in that person. And he paid! He paid the fare! Which, later, I laughed about that for a week. He paid four forty to rob a bus.

He came up by the glass and showed me the knife, low, by his leg, and he said, keep driving, don't stop, don't open the doors. And his hand — his hand was going like this. Shaking hard enough that I could see it in the mirror light. And I thought, okay. Okay, this boy has never done this. This boy decided to do this today, maybe an hour ago, maybe his mother's rent, maybe he owes somebody, and he's on a bus he can't get off of and he's realizing that right now.

So I kept driving. And I talked to him. I don't even remember what about, honestly. I think I asked him where he was going. Which is a comedy, right, I'm being robbed and I'm asking about his destination like a taxi. And he said Grajaú. And I said, ah, my cousin lived in Grajaú, by the station. And he kept the knife there but he started answering me. And there were eleven people on that bus. I counted, I always count, that's a habit, you count the heads at each stop. Eleven, and three of them were old. And my whole thought, the entire thought in my head, was: if this goes bad it has to go bad at the front. Not back there. So I kept him at the front, talking, and I drove, and I did not stop at the stops, which, by the way, the company loves that, that's a whole disciplinary form.

After about fifteen minutes he took the change bag and — there is no change bag, we're all card now, there was maybe thirty reais that a guy had given me for a favor, and he took it and he asked me to open the door at a place where there's no stop, on the bend, and I opened it and he got off and he said thank you. He said thank you, brother. I think about that. I think about that more than the knife.

And then, and this is the part — I finished the line. I finished the whole line. I dropped the eleven people at the end and did the return and put the bus in the garage at ten to one. Filled the paper. Seu Aparecido, the supervisor, he came out and he'd heard on the radio, and he stood there and he looked at the bus and he looked at me and he said, Edilson, and he put his hand on the back of my neck, and he said, you can take tomorrow. And I said no, no, everything's resolved, I'm on at four-fifty. And he said again, take tomorrow. And I said, Seu Aparecido, if I'm home I'm just going to be home, you know? And he laughed and let me go.

My hands started at the bus stop. On the way home, I'm a passenger now, sitting on somebody else's bus, and my hands went, and I put them under my legs and sat on them until Capelinha— until my stop.

I never told Bete. She was ten. What do you tell a ten-year-old? And I never told my mother, obviously, my mother would have sold her stove to buy me a helicopter.

And I did ask, a few months later, to move off the night lines to the morning, but that was a schedule thing, it was because of Bete's school hours, that's all that was. The 5119 is actually a good line. Good money, the extra. But the mornings work better with the house.
