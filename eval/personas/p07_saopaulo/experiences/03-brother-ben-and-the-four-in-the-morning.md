# Brother Bené and the four in the morning

_2015–2016, age 33_

So the church. People assume, oh, you're a believer, you were raised in it, no. My mother was Catholic in the way people are Catholic, which is the saint on the shelf and mass at a funeral. I went to church because of a man named Bené. Brother Bené. He drove buses for thirty-one years, the old lines, the ones with the fare collector and the turnstile that broke your ribs, and he was retired and his wife had passed and he lived two streets up.

And about eight, nine months after Simone went, I was very— I'm going to say I was tired. I was doing the shift and then doing five hours of extra at a warehouse in the madrugada, sleeping maybe three hours, and Bete was four and she had this thing where she woke up at night and came and stood by the bed, not saying anything, just standing there like a little ghost, and I would wake up and she'd be there in the dark. And I would lie there with my eyes open and feel — I'm not going to say what I felt, it's not useful. Let's say I was in a bad spot. Let's say there were some thoughts that I don't think came from the Lord.

And Bené knocks on the door at four in the morning. Four! An old man! And I open the door with a face like a dog and he says, come, there's coffee at the church, the prayer group, come. And I said I have to be at the garage at five-forty. And he said, so? The church is on the way. And it wasn't on the way. It was absolutely not on the way, it was the opposite direction, the man just decided geography was flexible that day. And I went. I don't know why I went. I think because he didn't ask me anything. He never once asked me about Simone. Not one time in five years. He'd just say, Edilson, sit there, and put a coffee in front of me.

And the prayer at that hour, look — it's still dark, it's cold in the way São Paulo is cold at five, which is not cold but it gets into you, and it's ten, twelve people, mostly old women, and they're praying out loud, all at the same time, which if you've never heard it, it sounds like a river. And I would sit in the back and honestly the first six months I mostly slept. Sitting up. With my eyes half open. I'm being honest with you, I slept in the house of God for six months and I think God knew I needed it more than He needed my attention. That was the most rest I got in that whole period of my life, that hour, in a plastic chair, in the dark, with those women making that sound. Glory to God, you know? Truly.

The pastor at the time was Pastor Almir, a very— he was a man of few words, ex-military police actually, which is a whole other conversation. And he had a way of saying my name, Edilson, like it was a full sentence. And I would have walked into traffic if that man asked me to. I never asked him for anything. Not once. He'd say, brother, do you need something, and I'd say no, pastor, everything's resolved, praise God. Everything's resolved. I said that so much it became a joke in my house, Bete says it now to make fun of me, she'll break a plate and go, everything's resolved, praise God.

There was one time. There was a family in the church, the husband had a stroke, young man, thirty-eight, and they took up a collection on the Wednesday and I put in the envelope. I put in all of it. All of it. It was a Wednesday and I got paid on the Tuesday, so, you know. Stupid. Not stupid — I don't regret it. But that month was rice and egg and I told Bete I'd eaten at the garage, which, the garage, we had a woman who sold marmitas but I wasn't buying any marmita, I was eating those cheap cream crackers, the ones that taste like a cardboard box, and drinking coffee, and telling a six-year-old that Daddy already ate. And she believed me because children believe you. That's the worst part of children. They believe you completely.

Anyway. That's the church. It's not, um— people think it's about doctrine, and honestly half the doctrine I couldn't explain to you, I've been going eleven years and if you asked me to find a book of the Bible I'd be there for a while, ha. It's that a man knocked on my door at four in the morning and lied about the direction of the church.
