# Timing Beatriz's route to the technical school

_This year, age 44_

Okay. Bete. My daughter. Fifteen. Fifteen years old and she's got a mouth on her like a lawyer, I don't know where it came from, certainly not from me, I'm the man who takes four minutes to order a coffee.

So she passed the exam for the ETEC. The technical school. She did the exam without telling me, that's the thing, she did the whole thing, the registration, the little photo, everything, on her phone, and then she came out one Saturday and put the paper on the table like she was serving me lunch. Informatics. And I — okay, I'm going to tell the truth, my first feeling was not pride. My first feeling came about eleven seconds later. My first feeling was: where. Where is it. And it's in Santo Amaro, which for you is nothing, forty minutes, but for me it's two conductions, it's the walk from the terminal, it's the bit by the overpass where there's nobody at six-forty in the morning, and she has some classes that end at ten past six in the evening. Ten past six. In June it's already night at ten past six.

So what I did — and she still doesn't know this and she's going to know if you play her this, so, hm — on my rest day I did the route. I did it as her. Left the house at the time she'd leave, walked at the speed a fifteen-year-old walks, which I had to guess, I walked slow, I'm sure I looked like a lunatic. Took the same bus. Then I did it again on a Thursday because Thursday the traffic is different. Then I did it a third time at night, the coming-back version, in the dark. Seven minutes from the terminal exit to the gate if you go by the avenue. Nine if you go by the back where the shops are, but the back has lights and people, the avenue has a wall on one side for about eighty meters. Eighty meters of wall. So I told her, go the long way, and she said, Dad, that's two more minutes, and I said, take the two minutes, I'll give you the two minutes, take them from me.

And she puts up with it, she's good, she sends me the little location thing. But she says, Dad, you're going to have a stroke before I'm twenty. She says that. Charming girl.

And look, I say — I say all the time, I want her to go far, I say it at church, I say, my daughter's going to university, she's going to leave this place, I want her in Campinas, I want her doing something with computers where she sits in air conditioning and nobody spits at her. That's what I say. And then in March there was a thing, an exchange program, six months in another state, Santa Catarina I think, and it was subsidized, it was basically paid, and I— I found reasons. I found a lot of reasons. The food would be different, the cold, who would she be with, and she looked at me for a long time and then she said okay, Dad, fine, and she went into her room. And she's never mentioned it again. Not once. And I know exactly what I did and I did it anyway. So, you know. Man of God, fifteen years, still an idiot on a Tuesday.

The money. I'll say the money thing quick because it's embarrassing. She said she didn't want the fifteen-years party. She said it's tacky, she said it's for people on the internet. Fine. So officially we didn't do a party, we did a lunch, twenty people, my mother made the meat. But I've still got the envelope. I've been putting into it since she was about eleven, and I didn't stop when she said no, I just — I kept putting. It's not much. It's for whatever. It's for a computer for the course, or if something happens, or, I don't know. And I've had months this year, with the tire and with my mother's medicine, where I should have opened that envelope and I ate badly instead for three weeks. Which is stupid. Which I know is stupid. If Bené were alive he'd slap the back of my head.

I get home at, depends, twelve-forty, one. And she leaves the hall light on for me, which drives me crazy because of the bill, I've told her a hundred times. And I come in and I go and I stand in her doorway for — not long. A minute. Just to hear her breathing, like when she was small. And then I eat standing up by the sink and go to bed and get up at four-ten.

And people say, Edilson, you're wrecked, you're exhausted, and I say no, no, everything's resolved, praise God. And it is. I mean it is. I've got a job with a card signed, I've got a daughter who passed an exam I didn't even know about, my brother didn't get to be twenty. So what am I going to complain about? Nothing. Nothing at all.
