# When Simone left

_2014, age 32_

Okay so this one is harder to tell because people want a villain and there isn't one. My wife — ex-wife, we never legally did anything, but in God's eyes and in the neighborhood's eyes she was my wife — Simone. She left when Beatriz was three. Three and four months.

And look, I want to say this clearly because my mother used to say ugly things about her and I would leave the room: Simone was not a bad person. She was twenty-four years old. Think about twenty-four. At twenty-four I was washing buses and stealing sleep in the back of a 6040 with a rag over my face. She had a baby at twenty-one with a guy who worked shifts, who was never in the house, who came home smelling of diesel and ate and slept and got up and went. And she was in two rooms with a child and a television. She used to say the walls were closer in the afternoon. That was her phrase. In the afternoon the walls come closer. And I would say, Simone, they're bricks, they don't move, ha, because I'm a genius, right? I'm a comedian. Instead of hearing what she said.

She had this thing where she'd do her nails at eleven at night. Every night. Perfect, with the little white line. And I understand it now, it was the only twenty minutes that belonged to her, but at the time I thought she was vain. God forgive me. She was drowning and doing her nails so she could feel like a person, and I thought she was vain.

The day itself was very undramatic. That's what nobody tells you. There was no screaming. I came off the shift and Bete was with the neighbor, Dona Cida, and there was a note but the note was almost nothing, it was like a shopping list, it said the pediatrician is Tuesday and she likes the blue cup and I'm sorry, I'm sorry, twice, and then her name. And I stood in the kitchen. And I remember the light in the hallway, the fluorescent tube, one end of it was going black — you know how they go black at the end before they die — and I thought, I have to buy a tube. That's what was in my head. I have to buy a tube, they cost eight reais, there's a place by the market. I stood there thinking about the tube for I don't know how long. Long enough that the neighbor came and knocked.

She went to Osasco, to a cousin. Then Curitiba, later. And here's the thing I never told anybody, not my mother, not the pastor, nobody, and I don't know why I'm saying it now — for about a year and a half I sent her money. Not much, a hundred, hundred and fifty, when there was overtime. And my mother would say that woman doesn't even send a diaper, and I'd let her say it. I'd sit there and eat my rice and let her say it. Because if I said no, Mother, actually I'm the one sending, she'd want to know with what money, and the answer was there wasn't any money, the answer was I stopped paying the installments on the sofa and the store came and took the sofa and I told everyone I sold it. That was the sofa story. Everybody in my family thinks I sold a sofa in 2015. I did not sell a sofa.

Why? People ask me why. I don't have a good answer. Because she was twenty-four and alone in Osasco. Because Bete's mother eating is Bete's business even if Bete doesn't know it for thirty years. I don't know. It's not nobility, don't put that. It was more like — it was something I could do. It was a task. When there's a task you can do you do it and then you're not standing in a kitchen looking at a light.

She calls Bete now, they talk, it's fine, it's cordial, she sent a jacket at Christmas and it was the wrong size and Bete wore it anyway for a week, in December, in São Paulo, in the heat, and I said nothing. Not one word. I made juice and I said nothing.

One detail, and then I'll stop. I don't use Terminal Capelinha. That's where I put her on the bus that Sunday when she came back for her things. And I know it's the faster terminal from my house, everybody says it, my supervisor says Edilson you're insane. But the Guarapiranga side has better connections for me and there's a guy there who does salgados that are honestly— I mean, the pastel, you have no idea. So I go the other way. It's twenty-five minutes more. Doesn't bother me.
