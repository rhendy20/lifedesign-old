# The night they brought Wellington home

_1999, age 17_

Is it— okay, the little light is red, so it's going. Good. So, you asked about something that made me who I am, and I'm going to tell you about my brother, because if I don't start there nothing else makes sense, you know? His name was Wellington. Wellington Ramos da Silva. He was nineteen. I was seventeen, going on eighteen in the September. He was two years older but honestly he was like six years older in the head, he was the one who talked, he had that thing, he'd walk into a place and inside of three minutes he knew everybody's mother's name. Me, I was the quiet one. Still am. My wife used to say talking to me was like paying a bill at the bank, you stand there and wait and eventually a little paper comes out. Ha. That's a good one, actually, that was a good one of hers.

So. That night. It was a Thursday, I remember because Thursday I worked at the bakery on Rua Décio, the bread came out at four in the morning, so I slept from about eight at night. The bread schedule, that was my whole life then — you go to bed when the sun's still orange, like a chicken. And my mother, Dona Marlene, she shook me and I thought I'd overslept, I thought, no, the oven, and she had this face. And I remember I looked at the clock first. Before her face, I looked at the clock. Twenty past ten. Then the rest.

He was at the corner of the little square, where the guys used to sit on the wall by the phone booth — there was a public phone there, the orange kind, you remember those, everybody's blood in the world was on that thing. It was not, look, I want to be honest, it was not a thing where he was innocent walking to church. He was in something. Not big. He was doing a thing for a guy, carrying something, I think he thought it was a two-week thing, he had this idea he was going to buy a van and do school runs, he really had that in his head, he'd talk about the van, he'd talk about the color of the van, he wanted white with a blue stripe, and he was thinking that this little job was the door to the van. That's what he was thinking. He wasn't thinking he was a bandit. Nobody thinks that. He thought he was a guy with a plan who was in a hurry.

The ambulance took fifty-one minutes. I know because I was standing there and there's a pharmacy sign that had the time on it, green numbers, and I watched the numbers. Fifty-one minutes from when my neighbor called. If you go from that square to the Hospital Campo Limpo, it's eleven minutes in traffic, seven if it's empty. Seven minutes. I could have — anyway. It wouldn't have. The doctor said after, no, it wouldn't have. But I did the math a lot of times, in that period. I knew every route. I knew you could take Estrada do Campo Limpo but if there's the market on Sunday you cut behind the school. I knew all of that at seventeen and I didn't have a car, I didn't even have a bicycle, what was I going to do with it, run?

What I remember most is his shoes. They gave us his things in a bag from the Extra supermarket and the shoes were in there and they were so clean. He cleaned them with a toothbrush, that guy, every night, the white part with a toothbrush and toothpaste, and it drove my mother crazy because he used her toothpaste. And I sat with the bag. That's, um. That's what I did, I sat with the bag, on the step, and a neighbor kept putting her hand on my head like I was six years old.

And people ask me did I want revenge, did I want to — no. No, I promise you no. I never had that. I'm not a brave man in that way. What happened was more like, after, I became very organized. Very. My mother said I got old in one week. I started writing things down, times, money, what needed paying. And I got the job at the bakery to full time and then I got a helper job at the bus garage washing them out, and that's the whole road from there to here, from that bag to me sitting in this apartment talking into a telephone like a crazy person.

One thing, silly thing. I don't go through that little square. There's no reason, it's just that if you come from the terminal the other way you catch the bakery and the pharmacy and you can buy your things on the same walk, so it makes more sense. It's maybe ten minutes longer but you resolve everything in one trip, you understand? So I go around. Everybody goes around now, honestly, they put a ramp in and it's ugly.
