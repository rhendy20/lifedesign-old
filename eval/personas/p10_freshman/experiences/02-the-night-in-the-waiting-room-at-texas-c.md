# The night in the waiting room at Texas Children's

_Sophomore year of high school, around 2022, and again since_

Um, so my sister has lupus. She got diagnosed when I was eleven, she was eight, and it's like, it's a whole thing, it's not one thing, it's like her kidneys sometimes and her joints and she gets these rashes and she gets tired in a way that people don't believe because she looks normal. People are always like 'but she looks fine.' Yeah. Okay.

But the night I'm thinking about is like, sophomore year? She had a flare and her creatinine was up, which is a kidney number, and they sent us to the ER at like eight at night and my dad was on a job in Conroe so it was me and my mom. And my mom's English is — she understands more than she can say. So like, at triage they ask you the questions fast, and there's a guy behind the glass, and he's going 'has she had any recent changes in medication,' and my mom is looking at me. And I'm doing the thing. I'm doing it in both directions. I'm telling him the prednisone dose and I'm telling her what he asked, and my mom keeps adding stuff in Spanish that's like, not what he asked, like she wants to tell him about how Cami didn't eat dinner, and I'm just going 'sí mami, ya se lo dije.' Which sometimes I didn't tell him. Sometimes I just said 'yeah I told him.' Because the line was long.

And then you wait. That's the main thing about it, you just wait, and the waiting room has that TV bolted up in the corner with the news on mute and captions that are always like three words behind. And it was cold, it's always so cold, and I got my mom one of those blankets from the nurse, and then she fell asleep sitting up with her purse on her lap, she never puts her purse down, and I went and got a Dr Pepper for a dollar seventy-five because the machine only took ones and I had ones.

And like, when the doctor came out around — it was late, like two? — she said some stuff about how they wanted to admit her and watch the kidney function and possibly do like a pulse of steroids, and she said the word 'nephritis' and she said something like 'this is the kind of thing that can get away from us.' And then I turned around and my mom's awake now and going '¿qué dijo? ¿qué dijo?' And what I said was, 'dice que la quieren dejar para observación nada más, para vigilarla.' Just for observation. To watch her. Which is true. That's a true sentence. It's just not the whole thing the lady said.

And my mom went 'ah, okay,' and she did the little sign of the cross and she relaxed. Like I watched her shoulders go down. And I remember thinking, okay. Good. And then I went and got her a coffee she didn't ask for.

I don't know. Like you kind of just become the person who knows where stuff is. Like I know where the good vending machine is and I know you park in the garage across, not the valet, and I know her insurance card is in the blue folder, the blue one, not the red one, the red one is my dad's truck stuff. And when Cami's actually awake and talking I'm kind of — I'm not that good at that part? Like she'll be in the bed on her phone and she'll go 'you can go home' and I'll be like 'nah I'm good' and then I'll just be standing there. And then I'll go fix her blanket even though the blanket is fine.

Um. She was in for six days that time. She's okay now, like, she's okay-ish, she's in remission-ish, they don't really say cured, they say 'stable.' And people ask me like, does that make you want to be a doctor, and I'm always like, no? I don't— no. I'm doing supply chain. Which everyone thinks is boring. But it's like, stuff has to get places and somebody has to know where it is. I don't know. That's not a deep answer. That's just the answer.
