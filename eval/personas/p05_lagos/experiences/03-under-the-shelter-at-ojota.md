# Under the shelter at Ojota

_2019, age 17-18, after JAMB_

Okay so this one is about how I became a nurse, and the honest version is not the version I give at interviews. 

At interviews I say, ah, I have always wanted to care for people, my grandmother was sick, I saw the nurses, blah blah blah. Half of that is true. My grandmother did die and there was a nurse at the general hospital in Ijebu who was very kind to her. Fine. But that is not what happened. 

What happened is I wanted medicine. That's what I wanted. I scored 268 in JAMB, which in this country for medicine is, you know, it's decent but it's not enough, and my post-UTME was okay but not okay-okay, and I didn't get it. And there was this whole period where people were saying, ehn, does your daddy know somebody, is there anybody in the school, my classmate's father was a lecturer somewhere and she got in with less than me. And my daddy — my daddy at that time, he could barely — you know. There was no somebody to know. 

So I was supposed to be deciding. Change of course, or wait one year and rewrite, or go for the private one where the fee is like three million a year which is a joke. And everybody had an opinion. My mummy wanted me to rewrite. Aunty Funmi wanted, I think she wanted me to do pharmacy actually. My daddy didn't say anything but I knew he wanted medicine because he had told his friends. That's the thing, he had already told his friends. 

So one day — this is the part I remember, this is the only part I remember clearly — I was coming from my aunty's shop in Ketu and I was at Ojota, standing under that shelter, and rain started. Not small rain. That Lagos rain where the gutter becomes a river and everybody just packs under one roof and you're smelling everybody. And there was a woman selling gala and La Casera and she had this nylon over her tray and she was still selling, in that rain, she was making change with wet hands. And I was watching her and my slippers were in water and my mind just — it went quiet. And I said, nursing. Just like that. Standing there. The rain hadn't even stopped. 

I didn't reason it. I want to be clear, there was no reasoning. It was like somebody came and put it in my hand. 

Then after, I built all the reasons. Oh, nursing is four to five years not seven. Oh, nursing you can travel with it. Oh, in nursing your progression is written down — you do your programme, you do your indexing, you register with the Nursing and Midwifery Council, you have a licence with a number. There is a syllabus. Nobody can come and say ah your uncle knows so-and-so. I actually liked that a lot. When I got the handbook in first year and I saw the whole four years laid out, courses, clinical postings, hours, I read it like a novel. My roommate was laughing at me. But you know what that is, that's — when you can see the whole staircase, you can climb anything. 

And I went to the cyber cafe at Ojota, that same week, and did the change of course myself. I paid with my own money. I didn't tell my daddy. I told him after, when the admission list came out, I brought the printout and I said Daddy, see, I got admission, and he looked at it and he said, nursing. And I said yes sir. And he said, that one is good, they will always need nurses. And he was quiet for a bit and then he said, are you happy, and I said yes sir. 

He never asked me again. Till today he has never asked me about the medicine thing. 

I think about that woman with the gala sometimes. I don't even know what she has to do with it, honestly. People will now say ah she saw a hardworking woman and she was inspired. It wasn't that. She was just there. It was just the moment my body decided something and then my mouth caught up later. 

That's how most of my big things have gone, if I'm honest. Which I don't tell people because it sounds mad. I tell them I'm a very analytical person.
