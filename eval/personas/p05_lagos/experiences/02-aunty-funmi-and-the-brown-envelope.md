# Aunty Funmi and the brown envelope

_2021-2022, age 19-20, entering nursing school_

So, Aunty Funmi. My mummy's immediate elder sister. She's the one paying my school fees, and I want to say from the beginning that without her I would not be where I am today, full stop. Let me say that first. Because sometimes when I talk about her people hear it wrong. 

She's in Magodo, her husband is in oil and gas servicing, their two children are in Canada, one is doing masters. Big house, two gens, one for the house one for the borehole. Dog called Bingo that hates me specifically. I'm serious, that dog is fine with everybody. 

So when I got the nursing admission my mummy took me there on a Saturday. We went in the morning and we sat in the car, in the compound, for — I'm not exaggerating — almost two hours, because Aunty was on a call. My mummy kept saying let's just be patient, let's just be patient. I was in my church clothes. It was hot. And when we finally entered she was so nice, ah, she hugged me, she said Temi the doctor, because she liked to call me that even after the medicine thing didn't happen. And she said don't worry about fees, just read your book. 

And she has done it. Every single session. Fees, accommodation, she bought me a laptop, HP, in my second year, and when my stethoscope was stolen she replaced it the same week. I keep all the receipts and the transfer alerts in a brown envelope in my box. I don't know why I do that. It's not like anybody will ask me. But I can tell you it's about one point two, one point three million a year when you add everything, and the laptop was two-fifty at that time. 

The conditions were never written down but everybody knows them. I call her every Sunday evening after church. I send my results, photographed, every semester, and if I don't send within two days she'll call my mummy, not me, my mummy. And holidays I'm in Magodo. Not resting o. Supervising the workmen when they came to fix the roof, going to market with her, cooking when she has her prayer group. That's fine, honestly that's fine, you can't eat somebody's money and be sleeping till ten. My mummy raised me. 

But sha there was one day — no, let me tell you the real thing. 

In 2022, my daddy's BP. He was at home and he just went, sort of, his speech was funny for like forty minutes and then it came back. My mummy was crying on the phone. Now, immediately, everybody's first idea is call Aunty Funmi. And I said no. I said give me small time. Because I knew what would happen — she would send the money, yes, she would absolutely send it, and then for the next one year every conversation will have that inside it. Every single one. So I called my coursemate Kemi whose uncle is at a private hospital in Ogba, and I did the referral myself, and I took my whole allowance from the placement, and my mummy sold — well. We sorted it. It was like eighty-something thousand for the scan and the drugs. 

And then after, after he was stable and discharged, I called Aunty and told her. And she was so angry. She said Temitope, am I a stranger? Am I a stranger in this family? Why didn't you call me? And I was saying sorry ma, sorry ma, everything happened fast ma. And she sent two hundred thousand that same night. 

Two hundred thousand. After it was already finished. 

I'm not complaining, God forbid. She's a very kind woman, I'm telling you, she has carried this family. When my mummy's freezer spoilt she bought another one. I just — I don't know how to explain it. It's like, when somebody is holding you up, you start to walk in a way that doesn't disturb their hand. You understand? You adjust your walking. And after some years you don't even remember how you used to walk before. 

Anyway. She's coming to my graduation, she's already asking about aso-ebi colours. I'll be there smiling, because honestly I will be smiling, she deserves it. She earned it. 

Ehn, and that dog. Bingo. Still hates me. Three years.
