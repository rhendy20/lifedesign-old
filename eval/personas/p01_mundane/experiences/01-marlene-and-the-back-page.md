# Marlene and the back page

_1999, age 25, four years into the job_

Okay so. Um. I want to say up front I don't really — I'm not sure I got the kind of thing you're looking for here. Like nothing's happened to me. I mean that in a good way. Tammy says that's a blessing and she's right. But you asked, so. There's this one thing I think about probably more than a normal person would think about it. It's dumb. It's from 1999. So, the county was coming off the old system, the AS/400 green-screen deal that Ron Mierzwa had basically held together with rubber bands since I think '83, and we were going over to this package from a vendor out of Indianapolis, I don't even remember the name of it now, Muni-something. And the way the conversion worked, they brought over your account master and your current read but they did not bring over your estimate history. Which sounds like nothing. Except on your compound meters, your three-inch and up, your commercials, the estimate logic was doing this thing where it would take the prior twelve and average it, only it was averaging on a calendar quarter and we bill on a — we billed on a rolling sixty-day cycle back then. So you'd get these commercial accounts where the estimate was coming in about eleven percent light, and then the true-up hits 'em in February and it's a fourteen hundred dollar bill for the Hillcrest Lanes or whatever and they're screaming at Doreen up front. And nobody caught it because everybody was looking at the summary page. The summary page balanced. It balanced fine. It's the back page, the exception register, it prints out on that greenbar and it's like nine inches thick and nobody reads it. I read it. I don't know why. I just always did. I'd take it home, honest to God, I'd take it home and go through it on the kitchen table after Tammy went up, because Nathan was a baby and he was up at eleven and up at two anyway so I might as well. And I found it. Took me, I want to say five or six weeks of Tuesdays to actually prove it, because I had to hand-figure like forty accounts to show the pattern. And I wrote it up. Two pages. And I gave it to Marlene Deemer, who was the supervisor then. And she — okay so Marlene was not a warm person. Marlene had been there since Nixon. And she read it, right there, standing up, took her maybe two minutes, and she put it down on the counter and she said, 'Kovarik, you're the only one in here who actually reads the back page.' And then she went to lunch. That's it. That's the whole thing. That's the — and I've thought about that sentence I would say weekly for twenty-seven years. Which is insane. My wife would tell you it's insane. She said it and went to lunch and I bet she forgot it by the time she got to her car. She passed, Marlene, she passed in 2014 I think, and I went to the funeral and I almost told her daughter about it and then I didn't, because it's — how do you explain that. 'Hi, your mom said one sentence to me in 1999.' You know. Anyway. So after that I started keeping my own set of books on it, basically. Green Bandy notebook at first and then when we got Excel on the machines I built out a workbook, and it's grown, I mean it's — it's got the exception logic in it, it's got the compound conversions, it's got the cross-reference for the parcels that got resplit in the annexation and never got a new account number. There's about eleven hundred of those. Nobody else can run it. That's not bragging, it's actually a problem, because I went to Myrtle Beach in, ah, 2016 with Tammy's family and they called me Wednesday morning from the office. And Thursday. On vacation. And I told 'em, I said guys, I'm on a beach. But I walked 'em through it. Twice. And I'm not — I wasn't mad about it, I want to be clear. Some of that's on me for building it the way I built it. And there's things in there that are, you know. There's a flag I put in for a certain kind of account where if the penalty kicks and it's a residential single-occupancy and the read history says it's an older person living alone — I zero the penalty. I just zero it. Nobody told me to do that. Nobody told me not to. That's not, I'm not trying to be a saint about it, that's just how the thing is supposed to work in my opinion. A lady on Social Security shouldn't eat a nine dollar penalty because her son didn't get the mail in. That's — anyway. That's my story. That's a sentence somebody said to me. Told you it was dumb.
