# Ardith's basement

_1996, age 22, first year on the meter route_

Alright, the last one, and then I'm — I feel like I'm hogging the tape here. So before billing I was on meters. Two years, ninety-five to ninety-seven, reading route, the west side and out toward the township. And this is before the radio-read stuff, so you're going in basements. You got the little handheld, the Itron, and you're going in people's basements with a flashlight all day. And there was a lady on Mulberry, Ardith Bolyard, and I say lady, she'd have been I don't know, seventy-eight? And her read comes in at, I want to say ninety-one thousand gallons on a sixty-day cycle, and she's a widow living alone in a fourteen-hundred square foot house. Her normal's like four thousand. So that's a leak, obviously. And the bill on that back then, with the sewer side, because we bill sewer off the water read, the bill was going to be eleven hundred and some dollars. Eleven forty, eleven forty-something. And I'm twenty-two, I don't know anything, but I know that's her whole — that's everything. So I go down there and it's the toilet. It's the flapper. It's a four dollar rubber flapper that had gone soft and it's running silent, it's not even making noise, it's just running down the back of the bowl. And she'd been hearing nothing. Couldn't hear it. So what I did, and I'd probably get walked out for this now, I went to the Hardware on Main on my lunch, got a flapper and one of them chain clips, one dollar ninety-nine and I think two forty, and I put it in. Took eleven minutes. And then I went back to the truck and I keyed the read as a meter-fail estimate. Which it wasn't. The meter was fine. I just typed it as a fail and put in her twelve-month average and it flowed through and she got a forty-one dollar bill and nobody ever said one thing to me about it, ever, in thirty years. And she gave me a can of Vernors out of her fridge. I didn't even want it, I don't like Vernors. I drank it. And I thought about that for probably a year, and what I ended up doing was I wrote up — I typed up a page, on the Brother, about doing a leak forgiveness deal, like a one-time adjustment where if it's a verified leak and you fix it in thirty days you get one cycle at your average, which by the way half the districts in the state do now and we do it now too, it's in the rate ordinance. And I typed it up. And I didn't put my name on it. I gave it to Ron Mierzwa and I said hey, somebody might want to think about this, and Ron put his name on it, which — I mean, I handed it to him with no name on it, so. That's on me. And it went to the board in ninety-eight and it passed. And Ron got a plaque. He's got the plaque, it's at his house, I saw it at his retirement thing. And I want to say clearly that I'm not sitting here eating my heart out about a plaque. I don't want a plaque. Where would I put it. And honestly if it'd had my name on it, a twenty-three-year-old meter reader, it goes in the drawer. It doesn't pass. Ron had twenty-five years and he golfed with a commissioner. So it was the right — that's just, you know, that's how you get a thing done. That's what I'd tell anybody. Ardith passed sometime in the early two-thousands. Her house is a rental now, they got that vinyl on it. But like eleven, twelve hundred accounts a year go through that adjustment now. I pulled the number once. Twelve hundred and six, that was 2019. Anyway. That's four dollars and thirty-nine cents of my own money, if you count the chain clip. So.
