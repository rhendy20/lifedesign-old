# Nine hours off Port Clinton with Denny

_August 2004, age 30_

Alright, uh, so the best day I ever had was probably August of oh-four, out of Catawba, me and my brother-in-law Denny. And I've told this story at the bowling alley probably eighty times but I tell it wrong, I tell the funny part. So. Denny had a nineteen-foot Lund, a nineteen-eighty-something, I want to say an '86, with a ninety on the back and then a little nine-nine kicker for trolling, and the kicker was the whole issue. Because Denny mixed his gas by eyeball. He would not measure it. I'd tell him, I said Den, it's fifty to one, it's not a suggestion, and he'd do the thing where he holds up the can and goes 'that's about right.' So we're out, I want to say four and a half miles northeast of the reef complex, and it's flat, it is glass, which for Erie in August is — you get maybe six days like that a year. And the kicker starts loading up. Four-stroke — no, two-stroke, sorry, it was a two-stroke, and it's loading up and fouling out, and we lost, I'd say we lost an hour and twenty minutes to that. And I pulled both plugs and cleaned 'em with — we didn't have a wire brush, I used the striker off a — I used a nail file out of his tackle box, I'm not kidding, and got 'em back in and we ran the rest of the day on one plug that was basically junk. Got seventeen fish. Kept our twelve. Big one was, ah, seven and a quarter, that was Denny's, and I have never let him forget that I netted it. So that's the version I tell. Here's the part I don't tell. Denny had got laid off at Cooper Standard in like, it would have been March. Fourteen years. Fourteen years he was there. And Karen, that's my wife's sister, Karen had told Tammy that he wasn't — you know, he wasn't doing great with it. Sitting in the garage. And Tammy said to me, she said, take him out on the boat, and if it comes up, let it come up. And I said yeah okay. And we were out there nine and a half hours. Nine and a half hours, two guys, twelve feet of boat. And it did not come up. Not once. Not one time. We talked about — we talked about whether to run a bottom bouncer or go to a crawler harness on a snap weight, we talked about that for I want to say forty-five minutes, seriously, forty-five minutes on a two-ounce versus a three, and we talked about the Indians bullpen, and at one point he said something about his dad's old Starcraft, and there was — there was a spot in there, right when the sun was getting low and we'd shut it down and were just drifting, and I had it in my mouth. I had the words in my mouth, something like, so how you doing with all that, or, you know. And what I said was, 'You ever try one of them drift socks?' And he said no. And I said they're about thirty bucks at the Dick's out in Findlay, and they'll hold you to like one-point-one. And he said huh. And that was that. And I'm not — look, I don't know that saying the other thing would've helped him any. He's fine, he's been at the Marathon terminal since oh-six, they're fine. And when we pulled the boat at the ramp, it's like nine o'clock, it's dark, and he's tying down and he doesn't look up and he goes, 'We oughta do this every year.' And I said, 'Absolutely.' And I meant it, I one hundred percent meant it when I said it. And we never did it again. Never once. Twenty-two years. It's not — there's no story there, nobody got mad, it's just, you know, the boat needed a lower unit and then Karen's mother had her hip and then his kids got into soccer, and it's like, you can't call a guy in March and go hey, remember that. You'd feel like a — I don't know. You'd feel funny about it. He's got a pontoon now. We see 'em Christmas Eve and the Fourth. He's a good guy, Denny. He really is. He still won't measure his gas.
